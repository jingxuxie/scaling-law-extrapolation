\begin{abstract}
Scaling laws are commonly fit on inexpensive pilot runs and extrapolated far beyond the observed range. We show that an excellent pilot fit need not identify the irreducible floor, asymptotic exponent, or compute needed to reach a target loss. In an exactly solvable family of infinite-dimensional linear-regression problems, positive mixtures of power-law target spectra generate exact Hurwitz-zeta risk curves. We prove two complementary lower bounds: a below-noise spectral component can change the asymptotic exponent and induce unbounded compute-to-target disagreement, while two ordinary floor-plus-power laws can match value and slope and remain statistically indistinguishable over a short log-scale span. We then introduce \ScaleCert, which returns a target interval or abstains. Unlike a finite exponent-grid plug-in, our continuous-exponent formulation covers every positive spectral mixture supported on a declared exponent interval; a convexity argument yields a finite linear program with an explicit $O(\Delta^2)$ grid correction. We also derive an exact bridge to trained regression: for $n$ Gaussian samples, the expected test risk of $M$-dimensional ordinary least squares is the spectral truncation risk multiplied by $(n-1)/(n-M-1)$, enabling unbiased debiasing. Across exact constructions, 90 noisy synthetic pilot problems, 5,000-draw finite-sample experiments, and 27 rolling-origin public-curve evaluations, conventional parametric intervals can be narrow but confidently wrong, whereas \ScaleCert\ exposes the coverage--informativeness tradeoff. Our results separate goodness of fit from extrapolation validity and turn structural assumptions into auditable certificates.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Scaling laws summarize how loss changes with model size, data, or compute and increasingly guide expensive training decisions \citep{hestness2017deep,kaplan2020scaling,hoffmann2022empirical}. A standard workflow trains several small systems, fits
\begin{equation}
  \Risk(M)=E+A M^{-\alpha},
  \label{eq:standard-law}
\end{equation}
and extrapolates to a target $M_\star$ outside the pilot range. Small residuals do not validate this step. Over a short log-scale interval, the floor $E$ and exponent $\alpha$ can be nearly confounded; a weak component with slower decay can also remain hidden and dominate only after the observed range.

Broken power laws improve fit when transitions are already represented in the data \citep{caballero2022broken}. Bayesian and prior-fitted extrapolators quantify uncertainty conditional on their curve priors \citep{adriaensen2023efficient,lee2025bayesian}. Conformalized scaling laws calibrate empirical residual intervals \citep{bukkapatnam2026conformalized}. These approaches are complementary to a more basic question: \emph{what target values are identifiable from a bounded pilot range under explicit structural assumptions?} Recent evidence of unreliable downstream scaling and ill-conditioned parameter--data fits makes that question timely \citep{lourie2025unreliable,kricheli2026coverage}.

We answer it in a linear-regression model where the risk curve is exact and laptop-scale experiments are exhaustive. Spectral assumptions already explain neural-like model, data, and optimization scaling in linear and random-feature models \citep{lin2024scaling,bordelon2024dynamical,lin2025improved}. We use the same language to construct statistically indistinguishable learning problems and to derive computable prediction sets.

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Exact realizability and lower bounds.} Positive spectral mixtures produce exact zeta-tail risks. We prove hidden-crossover and one-power floor--exponent lower bounds, including unbounded compute-to-target distortion.
    \item \textbf{Continuous-exponent certification.} \ScaleCert\ optimizes over an outer representation of all positive mixtures on $[\alpha_{\min},\alpha_{\max}]$. Its finite LP has simultaneous coverage and an explicit quadratic discretization correction; true exponents need not lie on the grid.
    \item \textbf{A trained-regression bridge.} We derive the exact mean test risk and debiasing factor for finite-sample Gaussian OLS, showing that the population spectral curve remains directly testable after training.
    \item \textbf{Stress tests beyond in-range fit.} Synthetic misspecification, finite-sample OLS, and public rolling-origin curves evaluate coverage, width, false confidence, and sensitivity to structural mismatch.
\end{itemize}
